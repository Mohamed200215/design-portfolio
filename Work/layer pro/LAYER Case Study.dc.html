Section 02 — Overview

What LAYER is

THE SHORT VERSION

Most skincare apps are built around discovery and shopping: quizzes, product recommendations, affiliate links. Almost none are built around the boring, essential part: actually doing the routine, in the right order, every day. LAYER is a stripped-down tracker that treats skincare the way a gym app treats a workout — as a routine you log, not a catalog you browse. Shopping does exist in the app, but on a separate branch: a personalised starter kit generated from your skin type, then search, bag and checkout, all deliberately kept out of the daily log.

WHY I MADE THIS

I already track my training and my macros closely, but my skincare routine lived nowhere. No order, no consistency check, no sense of whether it was actually working over time. If I wanted that structure for lifting and eating, why not for the one routine I was most inconsistent with?

CONSTRAINTS

One week, working solo, with no engineering support and no access to real product data. Everything here is designed to be judged as a prototype, not shipped.

DELIBERATELY LEFT OUT
  • An ingredient database and conflict warnings. Useful, but it turns the app back into a reference tool.
  • Progress photos. High effort per day, and the payoff is too slow to support a daily habit.
  • Real inventory sync. The low-stock warning is a designed state, not a working count.
LEAST SURE ABOUT

Enforcing a fixed step order makes the routine legible, but it assumes the user's order is correct. Someone with a dermatologist-set routine may find it rigid.

Section 03 — Problem

Buying the products is easy. Using them consistently isn't.

People end up with a shelf of products and no order to apply them in, no record of what they used, and no way to tell whether anything is working. Skincare apps answer the shopping question. Almost none of them answer the habit question.

01
No order to follow

Products arrive with instructions on the bottle, not as a sequence. Cleanser, serum, moisturiser, SPF — the order matters and nothing holds it.

02
Nothing gets recorded

Without a log there is no streak, no memory of last night, and no evidence to connect a change in skin to a change in routine.

03
Advice replaces tracking

Existing apps push recommendations and reviews. The daily act of actually doing the routine is left entirely to the user.

COMPETITIVE ANALYSIS

I looked at three categories of app, not only skincare competitors.

Skincare apps — Strong on product discovery, weak on daily-use tracking.

Fitness trackers — Hevy, Nike Training Club, the same two I compared in my dissertation. Strong on streaks, logging, progress-over-time framing.

Habit trackers — Strong on simple daily check-ins, weak on anything routine-specific like step order.

INFORMAL USER INTERVIEWS

Eight people, mostly men aged 20–30 who train regularly. Fifteen-minute conversations, either in person or over voice note, walking through what they own, what they actually do each day, and where it fell apart. Recurring findings:

  • Almost everyone had tried a routine and let it lapse within a few weeks.
  • The biggest friction wasn't knowing what to buy. It was remembering the order and doing it consistently.
  • Several explicitly compared it to missing gym sessions.
  • Six of the eight owned a product they had stopped using because they were unsure where it went in the order.
  • Nobody wanted advice in the app. They wanted confirmation they had done it.
  • PM was the session that got skipped, not AM. Tiredness, not forgetfulness.

"I'd never skip leg day, but I've gone a week without touching my serum and not noticed."

Key insight: the unlock isn't more product information. It's the behavioural hook that makes a gym log work — visible consistency, low-friction daily logging, and a sense of streak.
Section 05 — Personas

Two people, one habit gap

Both came out of the interviews. Neither needs a product recommendation. What they need is the routine to be legible and the doing of it to be recorded.

Primary persona

The Disciplined Beginner

Marcus, 24 — graduate analyst, trains 4–5× a week

Logs every set in Hevy and weighs his food, but his skincare is three or four products on a shelf used whenever he remembers. Bought them on a recommendation, never learned the order. Wants structure, not education.

Goals
  • A fixed AM and PM sequence he doesn't have to think about.
  • Evidence it is working, in the same shape as a training log.
Frustrations
  • Every app he tried opened on a quiz or a shop.
  • No idea whether serum goes before or after moisturiser.

"Just tell me the order and let me tick it off."

Secondary persona

The Relapsed Starter

Dan, 28 — trades shifts, trains when the rota allows

Ran a full five-step routine for two months after a breakout, then dropped it once his skin cleared. Six months on, half the products are out of date and he isn't sure which ones still work. Wants accountability past the flare-up, not another fresh start.

Goals
  • A reason to keep going on the nights he can't be bothered.
  • To know what he already owns before buying anything else.
Frustrations
  • Habit apps treat one missed day as a failed streak.
  • Progress is invisible day to day, so it feels pointless.

"It worked, so I stopped. That's the whole problem."

STEP 01

Onboarding

Three steps: skin type, concerns, products you already own. Skin type is stored as a Figma variable, so oily, dry and combination/normal each route to a different starter kit.

STEP 02

Build routine

Drag products into AM/PM order. The app enforces correct layering order automatically.

STEP 03

Daily log

One-tap check-off per step. The whole routine marks complete when every step is done.

STEP 04

Streak / progress

A simple day-count streak in the same visual language as a gym streak, plus an optional weekly progress photo.

STEP 05

Product shelf

An inventory of what you own, with a flag when something is running low.

STEP 06

Restock

Reached from the shelf or the starter kit: search with live results, product detail, a bag with quantity steppers and a price summary, then order confirmation.

THE SHOP BRANCH

Buying is real but deliberately off the daily path. It opens once from onboarding as a generated starter kit, and after that only from the shelf when something runs low.

Section 07 — Iteration

What changed, and why

Three decisions that did not survive first contact. Each one came out of watching someone use the prototype rather than out of the brief.

01
The AM and PM switch

The first build put morning and evening on two separate tabs in the bottom bar. Testers kept landing on the wrong one, because the app opens on whichever session is due and the tab did not move with it. The toggle moved up next to the heading, where it reads as a property of the routine rather than a place in the app.

v1 — separate tabs
v2 — inline toggle
02
Step completion

Checkboxes made the list feel like a shopping list, and hid the thing that matters most: the order. Numbering each step and filling the ring on completion keeps the sequence legible whether a step is done or not.

v1 — checkbox column
v2 — numbered rings
03
The streak

A running count in the header was easy to ignore and, on a missed day, felt punitive. Moving it to its own screen with the ring, the week strip and four-week completion gives the number context — a missed day reads as one gap in a pattern rather than a reset to zero.

v2 — progress screen
Section 08 — Brand

Brand identity

Clinical-but-warm: product-label typography and ingredient-list precision, structured like a lab, softened by warm neutral tones. Not another pastel wellness app, and not a streetwear drop.

MINIMUM SIZE

The mark holds at 30px in the app tab bar. Below 20px the interior linework closes up.

BRAND IN THE WORLD
TOKENS

Nine variables across two collections. Colors holds eight — background/bone, background/bone-dark, text/ink, text/ink-muted, accent/sage, accent/sage-tint, accent/clay, divider/silver — each with a light and a dark value. App State holds one string, skinType. No raw hex values anywhere in the file.

COMPONENTS

Ten component families, all variant-driven: Button (2 styles × 3 states), Step Row (4 states), Bottom Nav Bar (4 tabs), Progress Ring (5 percentages), Product Card (full stock / running low), AM/PM Toggle, Input Field, Reminder Toggle. A Status Bar and Home Indicator pair keeps the iOS chrome accurate across every screen.

DARK MODE

Built as a real Figma variable mode rather than a duplicated set of screens, so all 18 screens re-theme at once onto near-black #0A0A09 as the primary surface.

Display — Mont
Aa 0123

Geometric sans. Wordmark, section headers, editorial lines. Heavy 900 sets the wordmark. Two cuts only: ExtraLight 200 for anything large, Heavy 900 for the wordmark, nothing in between. Inside the app itself the display type is Fraunces Bold, set at 20–44px for screen titles and streak counts.

Body — Instrument Sans
Aa 0123

Neutral grotesk for body copy and UI. Set small, uppercase and letterspaced for step labels. Monospace handles hex values and data. The app UI runs on Inter Regular and Semi Bold, mostly at 12 and 14px.

DARK MODE

Dark is a variable mode, not a second design. Every colour in the file resolves through nine tokens, so all 18 screens re-theme from one switch and no component carries a hard-coded hex. The sage stays constant across both modes; the surfaces and ink swap around it. The one place it is not a straight swap: the completed-step ring. At full sage on a dark surface it read as an error state, so the dark mode fills it at reduced weight with a lighter tick.

Section 09 — Prototype & Testing

Prototype & testing

PROTOTYPE

The prototype runs the full path end to end, including the skin-type fork into the personalised starter kit and the search-to-checkout flow.

WHO

Five people from the original interview group. All train regularly, all own skincare products they use inconsistently.

HOW

Unmoderated on their own phone, then a ten-minute call. Think-aloud, no prompting until a task stalled for a minute.

TASKS

Complete onboarding, log a full evening routine, find why a product is flagged, and reorder it.

VIEW PROTOTYPE →
WHAT CAME BACK

Five problems, ranked by how many testers hit them. Four fed straight into the changes in Section 07.

01

Wrong session on open

Everyone who opened the app in the evening tried to log against the morning list. The session tabs did not follow the time of day.

4 of 5 testers

Toggle moved inline, beside the heading — Iteration 01.

02

Step order read as optional

With checkboxes, testers treated the list as things to do in any order. Two applied SPF before moisturiser and did not notice.

3 of 5 testers

Numbered rings replaced checkboxes — Iteration 02.

03

An extra tap to complete a step

The first build opened a step detail to mark it done. Every tester tried to tap the row itself first.

5 of 5 testers

One-tap check-off directly from the list.

04

Streak felt punitive after a miss

A bare counter in the header made a missed day read as failure rather than a gap.

2 of 5 testers

Streak moved to its own screen with week and four-week context — Iteration 03.

05

Low-stock badge missed on the shelf

The warning sat below the fold on the product card. Testers found it only after being asked to reorder.

3 of 5 testers

Unresolved. Noted for the next round.

Section 10 — Outcomes / Reflection

What this case study demonstrates

Reframing a saturated category by importing a mental model from an adjacent one I understand firsthand.

Building a system rather than screens: tokens, components and dark mode as a variable mode, so 18 screens re-theme from one source.

Research grounded in a real, personal pain point rather than a hypothetical brief, and a shop path added without letting it take over the daily job.

5/5

completed a full evening log unaided in the second round, up from two in the first.

3

taps to log a routine, down from seven in the first build.

6/8

interviewees owned a product they had stopped using because they were unsure of its place in the order.

9

tokens carry every colour across 18 screens and both modes.

What I'd do next: test whether the streak mechanic holds up past week 2–3 without becoming another abandoned habit tracker, and explore a lightweight accountability layer, without turning it into another feed.
WHAT I'D CHANGE ABOUT HOW I WORKED

I built the component library before I had tested anything, so the first round of findings meant reworking components rather than sketches. Next time the low-fidelity pass gets tested first and the system gets built around what survives. I would also test the shop branch, which never went in front of anyone.
